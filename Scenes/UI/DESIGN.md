## UI code (`Scenes/UI/`)
| File | Contents |
|---|---|
| `palette.gd` (`Palette`) | The few colours the game draws with in code rather than through a sprite. `INK`, `BONE`, `GOLD`, `EARTH_DK`, `SLATE` and the rarity ramp are `hexlib.py` hexes, so an outline matches the generated art it sits on; `PANEL_CREAM`, `SLOT_TAN` and `SLOT_TAN_DK` are the UI pack's, so a slot matches the panel art it sits on. `TEXT` and `TEXT_SOFT` are the pack's too (its outline brown and the label brown it letters FULL SCREEN in), and they took over from `INK` and `SLATE` on the cream on 2026-09-22: laid beside the pack's own screens the game's text was black and grey on cream where the pack's is always warm, and slate was the one colour the pack never puts there. `SLATE` is left with the common rarity step, which is semantic rather than decorative |
| `item_details.gd` (`ItemDetails`) | What one item is, written into a `VBoxContainer`: its name and rarity in that rarity's colour — with "· Two-handed" on that line where the piece takes both hands, a socket being as much of what it costs as its level is — then, given an `against` list, what wearing it instead would change, then its base stats and its modifiers. `deltas(item, against)` is that comparison as a plain Dictionary, so what it says can be checked without building an interface to read it off; it runs over the union of both sides' `effective_stats`, because a flat modifier can put a stat on one piece that the other has none of. `against` is a **list** because a swap does not always cost one piece: a greatsword costs the weapon and the offhand together, so what the deltas are taken against is everything `Equipment.displaced_by` names, added up. One piece is the ordinary case and reads exactly as it always did. Leaf for a gain and rust for a loss — every stat the game has is better the larger it is, so the sign carries the whole meaning. The bag and the fight's panel both fill from here, so an item reads the same in both. Wants a bone background — the darker half of the rarity ramp was picked to be read on the white panel, not on wood |
| `item_slot.gd` (`ItemSlot`) | One item as a square: its icon on a flat tan socket, and above common its rarity's drawn frame (`ItemRarity.frame`, from `tools/item_frames.py`) laid over it. The UI pack draws an inventory slot as exactly that socket — one flat square with a gutter around it and nothing else — so the `StyleBoxFlat` is the art rather than a stand-in for it; the frame is ours, the pack having no notion of one. `BagPage.SLOT_GAP` keeps the pack's seven-to-one square-to-gutter proportion. Both places that show an item use it — the bag's grid and the panel at the end of a fight — so a rare piece looks the same in each |
| `coins.gd` (`Coins`) | The coin, and how many of them a purse is worth. `Assets/coin4_16x16.png` is a spin, and its geometry is **measured**: the sheet is 180 px, which is eleven 16 px cells with 4 px of slack, and the last two cells are empty -- so `FRAMES` is 9 and the sheet's width says nothing useful. `icon()` is the first frame at rest, which is what the bag's footer and the fight's verdict wear; `frames()` is the looping spin, which only the coins in the air use -- a widget that never stops moving is furniture that fidgets. `count_for(amount)` is 1 + log10 floored, counted in whole tens rather than written with `log()`: `log(1000) / log(10)` is 2.999999999999999 in doubles, and floored that is a thousand gold paying three coins instead of four |
| `kill_pips.gd` (`KillPips`) | How far through a fight the player is, as a row of pips -- a pip an enemy, **its shape its tier**: a round pip for the rabble, a bone skull for an elite, a crowned skull for a boss. The bar starts lit and each kill spends the **leftmost** pip, so it drains left to right and the skull or crown parked at the far end is the elite, or a settlement's boss, still to be fought. **A spent pip keeps its shape** in dark greys one step above the ink, so a dead elite still reads as a skull and the bar still says how many there were. The same row stands on a bounty board's heading (`show_board`): a round pip lit for each posting handed in, and in the last place **a gift** -- a box under a red bow -- lit while a cleared board's reward waits, so the row reads as the way to a reward. The user picked the gift over a star, a gem, a trophy, a money bag and a medal, after turning down a chest, whose lid and seam, dark, read as an envelope: the last place is dark most of the time, so it is chosen by its silhouette. **The shapes are the user's (2026-10-03):** they drew separate ovals with a skull among them and a bigger mark at the end, picked the small skull and the Scowl's brow for the boss out of the options, asked for bone on every skull, then for round pips and **one small size for every bar**. Why one size: a big set (11 px) made a settlement's fifteen 193 px wide, which runs under the character panel on the default 576-pixel window. So every sprite is 9 px across, the round one centred in it and standing two pixels from the next with no gap between sprites. **And no bar shows more than ten** (`SHOWN`, the user's next call the same day): past ten it shows the nine from the enemy being fought and a caret pointing right (`MORE`, "more incoming"), the one just killed dropping off the left at each kill, until the last ten are in view -- then the caret goes and those ten drain where they stand, so the boss is only ever drawn once the player can reach it in ten. That keeps every bar at 90 px at most, well clear of the character panel (`NAME_ROOM` is back at 88). `width_for(slots)` is `mini(slots, SHOWN) * WIDTH`, arithmetic rather than a measurement because the HUD sizes its clock to match before the pips exist; `_init` asserts every sprite is still `WIDTH` across. A bar is as tall as the crown whether or not one is in it, so the clock under it never moves. `_init(slots)` settles the length once, ten by default (`SLOTS`): a tile fight is the lineup draining once, a farm run is the cycle between elites refilled when that elite falls, the dungeon its block of fifteen floors. The tier at a slot comes from `Encounter.tier_in`, which `HealthBar` reads too, so the pip standing for an enemy and the frame over its head never disagree; a floor before a descent began is drawn as rabble, since asking the lineup for a negative place would read it from the end. *(Until 2026-10-03 the bar was the capsule cut from "Pixel UI pack 3", doubled to two panel pixels a sprite pixel and drained to the pack's undivided grey; beside a line of body text on the board it looked clumsy, and an undivided run could not say how many were left to do.)* |
| `health_bar.gd` (`HealthBar`) | The health of whatever is standing in front of the player, as a bordered trough wearing the frame its tier earns: a plain ink wall for a common, a cut green gem bracketed into each end for an elite, a three-pronged gold crown for a boss — and the rail above and below the channel changes with it too, one smooth run, then divided into links, then divided and beaded, so the tier is read along the whole bar rather than only at its ends. The parts are **generated, not cut** (see the sprites section): the bought packs draw a flat capsule and a glossy sheared bar and nothing with an elite's frame or a boss's. Assembled from three parts at runtime the way `KillPips` is, and for the same reason — one sprite would fix the bar's length — at the same `PIXEL`, because the two stand in one column. The **fill stays drawn**: a `ColorRect` in `FILL`, which is where the game's health red now lives (`CombatScene` borrows it back for its crit numbers and the end of the clock's ramp). A sprite fill would arrive in fixed steps, which is what the bought bars were turned down for; it is snapped to whole sprite pixels all the same, and never to nothing while anything is still standing, because an enemy on its last hit point has to show a sliver. Behind the red a pale **ghost** (`GHOST`) marks what blows took off: it holds `GHOST_HOLD` then drains at `GHOST_SPEED`, and only a ghost that has caught up starts a new hold, so steady attacking never pins it. `TROUGH` is the same in all three tiers — a cap's last column is a channel column, so the ornament grows **outward** and the channel never moves, which is what lets a full bar mean one thing whoever walked in. `_init` asserts all of that off the textures, the way `KillPips._init` checks its parts add up |
| `orb_slot.gd` (`OrbSlot`) | One orb as a square in the bag's tray, on the same tan socket an item sits on and with no rarity ring, because an orb has no rarity. Its own class rather than a mode on `ItemSlot`, because the two differ in the thing that matters most about a square: an `ItemSlot` takes **no mouse input at all** so the drag that scrolls the bag survives a press beginning on one, and an orb square is the opposite -- the click target and the hover target both, standing in the footer where there is no drag to protect. `SIDE` is 24 and `ICON` 16, which is half the 32 the icon is cut at: six squares and five `ORB_GAP`s come to `BagPage.WIDTH` less under a gap, and a 2:1 step keeps a source pixel a square pair on screen. Three states, because three things can be true of an orb and all three have to read at a glance in a row of six -- never found is the icon at `ItemSlot.EMPTY_MARK_ALPHA`, held but useless against the open piece is modulated to `DIM` grey, and held and usable is full colour, darkening under the cursor. The count is drawn only past one and **hangs off the bottom-right corner** by `COUNT_OVERHANG`, in the body font with an ink outline: it was Pixellari 16, which is two thirds of a 24 px square and covered half the gem even hung off the corner (changed 2026-09-25) |
| `orb_card.gd` (`OrbCard`) | What an orb is, while the cursor is over it, on the cards' cream `TextPanel`: its name in `TEXT`, what it does in `TEXT_SOFT`, and a status line -- `Not found yet`, or for a super orb `Use on <piece>` in leaf or the refusal from `SuperOrbTable.why_not` in rust (an ordinary orb is never spent on the open piece, so it says nothing of it; until 2026-10-01 it did). That third line is the one worth having: a player can see that a square is grey, and what they need told is what would have to be true for it not to be. **Not Godot's tooltip, on purpose** -- see the gotcha below. It is the UI layer's **last** child, because it is placed at the square's own x and is wider than the right-hand end of the tray has room for: it hangs out over the character sheet, and a `CanvasLayer` draws its children in tree order |
| `loot_beam.gd` (`LootBeam`) / `loot_beam.gdshader` | The beam standing over a find lying in the arena, in that piece's ring colour, after the user's references (Old School RuneScape's drop beams and a clip of tiered beams with ground rings): a **thin** pillar with a white-hot core and a coloured glow, tapering to a point, over a flat ring round the find's foot. Two layers, so the find stands *inside* the light: the pillar and the far half of the ring behind it, the near half of the ring in front. **The foot of the beam is a column round the find** (the user asked for the beam to surround it): a tube as wide as the ring, its far wall behind the find and its near wall in front, faint in the middle and bright towards its edges -- which is what makes a flat shape read as round -- with streaks of light circling it, and it dissolves as it climbs into the thin pillar. Its edges fade faster than its body, or it read as a glass jar with a lid. **Each rarity adds something rather than only growing** (`LOOKS`), so a unique is told from an elite at a glance: rare is the beam and the ring; elite winds a ribbon up it and throws sparks; unique is taller and wider, with two ribbons half a turn apart and a second ring pulsing out -- a ribbon's turns are drawn on whichever layer they are on, so it winds round the find. Two attempts before it were turned down: a flame sprite cut from a bought pack (a 16 px flame scaled up only gets blurrier, and cannot grow with the rarity) and fat dithered flame tongues after the user's sketch, which read as fire rather than as a beam. A shader rather than a sheet so one piece of code draws every size. **It is drawn as pixel art** (the user's ask, 2026-09-30): at first it was whole pixels of the find it hangs off, which at `ui_scale` 2 is finer than the backdrop's and every fighter's pixel, and its see-through ramps read as a glow painted over the art rather than a part of it. Now its pixel is the fighters' (`CombatScene._snap`, handed to `make` as `pixel`), a pixel is drawn at one of two strengths and never a ramp between, and it is in three colours -- the ring colour, the step above it on its own ENDESGA 64 ramp (`HOT`, picked by hand: the palette colour nearest a lightened lilac is a grey) and white. A fading light is a 4x4 ordered dither between the two, but only from `FAINT` to `SOLID`: dithered all the way down, the column's faint near wall laid a haze of dots over the find and the rare pillar's glow thinned to a dotted line. Fully opaque, with the faint cut to nothing, it lost the tube round the find and hid what stood behind it; the user asked for the tube back, near transparent, and for the whole beam to be see-through, so a lit pixel is `OPACITY` (0.7) and faint light a flat `HAZE` (0.2) of whole pixels. The line thicknesses in the shader are in beam pixels (`* pixel`), or a ring thinner than one would break into gaps on the coarser grid. Rare and better only -- an uncommon ring is the hundredth of its kind -- and the three best orbs, which borrow a rarity's look and colour from their `OrbTable` row. It shoots up once the find has landed rather than riding the arc |
| `skill_slot.gd` (`SkillSlot`) | One skill: the pack's framed 16 px icon drawn at 2x with no socket behind it (the icon brings its own frame), and `rank/max` hanging off its corner as `OrbSlot`'s count does. Four states: locked wears the tree's Locked mark, open-and-unlearned is `OrbSlot.DIM` grey, learning is full colour, maxed is ringed in `Palette.GOLD`. It takes the mouse and passes every press on -- whether a point goes in is `Skills`' decision, and a refused press still leaves the card saying why |
| `skill_tree_view.gd` (`SkillTreeView`) | One tree placed by hand on `SkillTree`'s grid, with the lines drawn in its own `_draw` so they run under the icons: ink, or gold where the parent has a point |
| `skill_card.gd` (`SkillCard`) | `OrbCard`'s twin for a skill: name, what one point does, what the points in it add up to, and a status line (`Click to learn`, or `SkillTree.why_not` in rust; none at all for a skill at its most, whose ring and count already say it). Not a tooltip, for the reason in the gotchas |
| `bag_page.gd` (`BagPage`) | The bag against the left edge, the character sheet beside it and the orb tray at its foot, as one Control: showing or hiding it opens or closes all three. Owns the selection, the doll and its hand-placed `DOLL_SOCKETS`, the comparison, orb crafting (`_craft_rng`) and the orb card, and saves the inventory after every change it makes. `open()` resets to the grid, `refresh()` redraws from the inventory keeping what is open, `refresh_gold()` and `refresh_orbs()` are the cheap per-kill calls, `layout()` fits it to the window, `closed` is its X. The design reasoning is in `Scenes/DESIGN.md` |
| `skills_page.gd` (`SkillsPage`) | The skills page, built the same way: free points over the two `SkillTreeView`s, a Reset per tree, and the `SkillCard` beside the hovered skill. `open()` redraws it from the inventory |
| `juice.gd` (`Juice`) | Static one-shot effects shared by the fight and the map: `burst` (a fading puff of untextured square particles that frees itself), `hit_stop` (sets `Engine.time_scale` for a real-time moment; the **one that ends last wins** -- a call ending sooner than the running one is dropped -- so a kill's freeze cannot cut short a rare find's slow-motion, and `recover` eases it back to full speed rather than snapping), `shake` (whole-pixel rattle of a `Node2D` about its home) |
| `shine.gdshader` | A diagonal glint sweeping across an icon (`ItemSlot` tweens it through `SHINES` sweeps as the square is drawn, then it lies still), in whole source pixels, worked from the node's own pixels rather than UV because an icon may be an atlas region. `ItemSlot` puts it on rare and better |
| `ui_theme.gd` (`UITheme`) | Builds the `Theme` at runtime from `Assets/UI/ui_sheet.json`, the way `HexTileset` builds the TileSet. Each sprite becomes an `AtlasTexture` in a `StyleBoxTexture` with **that sprite's own** nine-slice margin, read from the JSON, and tiled (never stretched) axes — a panel's frame and a button's bevel are not the same thickness. One `FONT_COLOR` and one `DISABLED_FONT_COLOR` cover every button, because every lettered face is the pack's green replayed in the icon buttons' brown (red for danger, grey when disabled), lettered in the same cream as their marks (`Palette.PANEL_CREAM`). `UITheme.theme()` returns the shared theme; use it through the type variations `WoodPanel`, `TextPanel`, `HeaderBar`, `PanelLabel`, `WoodButton`, `WoodDangerButton`, `LightButton`, `LightDangerButton`, `BrownIconButton`, `BareIconButton` and `CloseButton`. The last three are all icon buttons and they are not the same kind: `BrownIconButton` (`ICON_FACES`) is a face like the lettered ones, stretched to whatever it holds, but padded equally on all four sides (`ICON_FACE_MARGIN`), so one wearing a square mark comes out square -- and brown rather than green because the corner buttons that first used it stand on the map and are places to go rather than actions to take. `CloseButton` is the odd one: an icon rather than a stretched face, so it has no texture or content margin and no pressed sink (the sprite holds it), and `UITheme.icon_size` says how big to make it — a Button with no text and no padding has no minimum size of its own |

Set `theme_type_variation` on a plain `Button` and Godot drives hover, press and disable itself; `wood` buttons are meant to stand on `WoodPanel`, `light` buttons on `TextPanel`. The main scene scales its panel by `ui_scale`, so UI pixels are a whole multiple of the screen pixel like the map's.

## Why there are two fonts
Pixellari renders at 16 px and nowhere else, and at `ui_scale` 2 that is a 32 px line in a 648 px window: eighteen lines a screen, every modifier wrapping, a town's three panels covering the map. Lowering `ui_scale` halves the art too and shrinking Pixellari breaks its glyphs, so body text got its own font instead: **Ark Pixel 10 px proportional, latin** (OFL, `Assets/ArkPixel10-OFL.txt`), 7 px capitals, imported like Pixellari (no antialiasing, no hinting, no subpixel positioning). It pads itself to a 14 px line, so `UITheme` wraps it in a `FontVariation` that takes `SMALL_FONT_TRIM` off the top and bottom and leaves 11. Turned down: Tiny5 at 8 (5 px capitals, the user found it too small to read), Scientifica at 11 (its m reads as an H, which is no good when the commonest word is Damage, and its % is a speck), Cozette at 13 (barely shorter than Pixellari), Jersey 10 (only lands on its grid at 20), Silkscreen (no lowercase), Pixelify Sans (not a bitmap face). Names, headings and button faces stay Pixellari, so a block reads as a big coloured name over small lines. The same pass put an open piece's three stacked buttons in one row behind a back arrow, gave the town's counters marks instead of words so all their tabs stand in one row, and turned the tile panel into a corner card for a while (it went back to a full-height column on 2026-09-17, was a card as tall as its rows for an afternoon on 2026-09-25, and is a full-height column again: see `Scenes/DESIGN.md`).

## The review of 2026-09-30
The user asked for a review of every screen and every string and picked what to fix. Some of the reasoning:
- **A pressed button's word never rose.** The kit's screenshot drew hover and pressed as a `Panel` with a centred `Label` -- a stand-in no button is -- so the word sat higher there than on the real face, which sinks it a pixel on press (`UITheme.build`). The game needed nothing; the kit now draws real buttons wearing each state's box.
- **An arrow means Equip will take it.** `BagPage.is_upgrade` asked only the stats, so a level 12 sword wore the arrow over a greyed Equip. It asks `Inventory.can_equip` too.
- **A modifier is a sentence, on one row.** Name over value doubled a card's height for rust lines that read naturally as "+8% increased Damage"; the value under the name was there only for the detailed band, which a one-row sentence wraps just as well. It is what gave the smith's counter room for the piece, with his base-stat table left to the hover card.
- **The collection's unlocked-but-unfound square is grey, not darker.** The user had lifted `SHADOW` from 0.35 to 0.65 because the unfound were "way too dark" to make out; at 0.65 they read as found. Colour, not light, is what now says "not held", and the card says "Not found yet".
- **Achievements got a bar a square** so the page no longer reads as a second collection log: a square there is a thing being worked at.
- **A banner waits behind a question.** Raised over a pop-up it lay across the pop-up's title; it now goes up once the window is clear.
- **Hotkeys wear the keyboard pack's keys** (the user's ask), on the tip card after the page's name, so the corner's tooltips are plain names now rather than descriptions.
- **The interface is in ENDESGA 64** (the user's call), the palette the fighters and the backdrops were already drawn in, so the chrome, the words and the art beside them are one set of colours. The pack's chrome was mapped by hand (`tools/ui_kit.py` `UI_FIXED`) and everything else to its nearest in OKLab, keeping apart the shades of a bevel or a ramp -- but only into a colour of the same family: the first pass let a brown step away into a teal of the same lightness and turned the wood panel green. ENDESGA offers two creams; the user took the pinker f6ca9f over the paler f9e6cf for more between panel and slot, and that moved every word's colour: the text roles were re-picked to 4.5:1 on it (green, blue and red a step darker) and a unique's name became vermilion, since no gold in the palette reads on the cream. A dead button is a pale tan face now, not grey: the grey was the one cold colour in the interface. A lost fight is headed red.
- **The copy's house rules**, from the same review: one word for a thing -- an equipment *slot* (not socket or place), *discard* (not throw away), *kills*, *Spawn Speed* (not "walk in"), *crit* (the stat labels' word), *item* (not piece or find); British spelling (Armour, as Favour and Sceptre); sentence case on headings and banners; a flat bonus to a percentage stat says "to" ("+5% to Crit Damage") so it is told from "increased"; **no full stop on anything shown as a tooltip** (a button's reason, a mark's note), a sentence on a panel keeps its own. The item rarity once called elite is shown as **Epic** (`ItemRarity.LABELS`; saves still say "elite"), since "an elite sword" read as one an elite had dropped. Tile modifiers whose names clashed became Dusk (with the curse Short Days), Brutal (with the elite prefix Savage) and Miserly (Timeless read as a boon).

## Phones, tablets and the web, either way up (2026-09-30)
The user asked for the game to be playable on a phone, a tablet and in a browser, held either way up, by changing the layouts, and ruled on four things: the touch input it cannot be played without is in; held upright a town is its page over the bag; held upright the corner buttons are a row along the foot; and the renderer and the exports are a later, separate step.

**Why the stretch went.** The project stretched the 1152x648 window with `canvas_items` / `expand`, and the code drew its own `ui_scale` 2 on top. Any window but exactly that size was then scaled by a fraction -- a maximised 1080p monitor by 1.67, a phone on its side by about the same -- which broke both "whole-number scales only" and "Pixellari only at 16 px", and a phone held upright drew the base at 0.94x, body text about 6 dp tall. So a real window is now drawn at its own pixels (the main scene sets `CONTENT_SCALE_MODE_DISABLED` as it starts) and `ui_scale` is picked from it: the largest whole number that still leaves today's budget, 576 x 324 panel pixels, on the long and short sides (`UITheme.pick_scale`). The default window picks 2, so nothing on a monitor moved; a phone picks 3 either way up, so turning it keeps the scale. A headless run keeps the project's stretch: with it off, headless reports a 64 x 64 window, and every test lays out against the 1152 x 1152 one it has always had. The scale is picked once, at start-up -- a browser resized mid-game keeps it, and every page lays itself out again in the new room.

**Why there are only two layouts.** With the scale picked that way, any window on its side is at least 576 wide and 324 tall, which is the window the game was already budgeted for, so it lays out exactly as it did. Only a window held upright is left narrower (324 to about 430 across on phones), and that is the one other layout (`UITheme.narrow`): what stood side by side stands one over another. The pages dock into a room the main scene hands them (`area`), so the one place that already knew which pages there are is the one that decides what shares the screen.

**What goes where, held upright.** Every page, and the tile panel, is a sheet along the foot over the corner row: centred at its own width, as tall as what it holds and never more than half the window, scrolling past that (the user's call, 2026-10-01, after a review of every screen at 360 x 780: "panels don't need to be full height, just render what you can"). They first stood full height, which left a column of empty cream under a short page. **Full width was tried and turned down the same day** (the user: "this looks much worse"): the sheets ran the window's width, the scrolls stretched what they held and the grids of squares became flows filling it, eight across in the log -- and the tile panel had run the window's width the round before, at the user's own first ask. Each is its own width again. A flow (the skill trees) knows how tall it is only once it has laid out, a frame after it was resized, so a sheet is stood again the next frame (`UITheme._settle`). Across a monitor nothing of this applies, and the tile panel stands full height there, the user's 2026-09-25 ruling. A tile chosen under the sheet is lifted into the middle of the map above it, so the sheet never hides what it describes. A banner off a fight stands under the character panel, which at that width it otherwise covered. The corner buttons, a column because beside a full-height page there was height to spare and no width, are a row for the opposite reason, and a thumb reaches the foot. A town is its counter over the bag, the counter giving up whatever the bag needs (`BagPage.least_height`: two rows of the grid, and the doll at the smith), each a sheet at the foot of its share, so a short counter stands right over the bag. The doll's sheet stands centred straight over the bag's -- on a transcension's screen too. The skill trees stand one at a time between a previous and a next arrow, centred, with nothing to scroll -- they wrapped two over one at first, and the user found scrolling a tree off. The two choice cards and the cloud's two saves stack, the curses' two sentences narrow and wrap, the dialogue's words narrow, and a card with no room either side of its square goes under it.

**The fight.** Fighters were sized by height alone, which held upright made them as tall as the window is wide; they now take the lesser of the height share and `ACTOR_WIDE` of the width. They also never went finer than one backdrop pixel, and a backdrop covering a tall window has a big one, so held upright they snap to half of it -- still one grid. Held upright the top-centre column stands under the character panel (`hud_top`, the main scene's), and the fight itself stands in the middle of the window (the user's call, 2026-10-01: at the foot it was too low): the backdrop, covering the window's height, put its ground line 86% of the way down, so the scene is lifted until the feet stand at 60%, the ground's own bottom rows repeating under it to the window's foot and the nameplate under the fighters rather than at the foot. The cave's bottom rows hold a ledge, so down there the fill is striped. The fight's corners and the character panel keep to a phone's safe area.

**Touch, the minimum.** Godot turns a finger into a mouse, but a finger has no hover: a press muted the item card, so no piece's card could ever have been read on a phone, and every tooltip would have come up after every tap. So under a finger (`Cursors.touched`) a tap shows the card and the worn piece's beside it (there is no Alt), a held finger shows a tooltip without pressing the button, a first tap on a skill or an orb reads it before a second spends it, a finger may wobble further before a tap is a drag, two fingers pinch the map's zoom a whole step at a time, the X answers in a finger-sized box on a touchscreen, Android's back button is Escape, and a tap where an aimed spell can do nothing puts it away (a phone has neither a right click nor Escape).

**Found on the way:** a run's loot popup with the full-bag warning ran its title off the top of the default window. A popup taller than the window now gives up rows of its finds, which scroll (`DropsView.fit_rows`), and a popup shrinks back to its contents when a find opened in it is shorter than the grid was. The review at 360 x 780 also found the bag's questions standing under the town page held upright -- a page now comes to the front with its question -- and a tooltip left behind where its control had been when a popup settled after it came up; a tooltip now follows its control.

**Left for later:** the dials `TOWN_SPLIT`, `ACTOR_WIDE` and `LEAST_ROWS` are unplayed. Held upright at the smallest budget, the smith's counter keeps its tabs and its two buttons but has no room left for the piece's lines; a taller phone, or the doll folded away, gives it back. ## Rules and gotchas
- **A floating panel cannot be a Godot tooltip, and that is why `OrbCard` is not one.** A tooltip is its own popup window, so it cannot inherit the bag panel's `ui_scale` transform; the only way to make it match the panel beside it would be to ask for type at `FONT_SIZE * ui_scale`, and by the rule above that comes back interpolated next to art that is not. So a card like this is an ordinary Control on the scene's own `CanvasLayer`, scaled by `ui_scale` and placed by hand -- which is what `CombatScene`'s full-bag warning already is, for the same reason.

## Naming the character (2026-10-01)
The user asked for a pencil that renames the character through the platform's own text input. It went to the character page's
bar, beside the name, rather than the corner panel: the user had just had the panel shortened, and a pencil in its band
made it some twenty panel pixels longer on the map (a badge on the portrait was the other option shown; the user chose the page).
The text box is `DisplayServer.dialog_input_text`, the system's own, so there is no text field of the game's to build or
to dress; where a platform has none (`FEATURE_NATIVE_DIALOG_INPUT`: Linux, the web) the pencil is not built, and a text
field of the game's own is the thing to add if one of those ships. The pencil is drawn in `tools/ui_kit.py` like the
flag and the sack, in the brown cut so it reads dark on the green bar as the name does.
